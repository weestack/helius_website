Open source

No walls

Plenty of infrastructure companies describe themselves as open source. Fewer will write down what that means in terms you could hold them to. This page is the attempt.

My pledge

I deliver, you own 100%

This is the part of the site I would most like to be judged on. Each of these is a specific, checkable commitment rather than a value statement — if I break one, you can point at it.

  1. 01

    Everything in your repository

    Pipelines, manifests, Terraform, dashboards, alert rules, runbooks. Committed to repositories you own, under a licence you pick. Nothing lives only on my machine or in an account I control.

  2. 02

    Open-source components only

    Every tool I put into production is open source and self-hostable. Where a managed service genuinely is the right answer, I say so and you decide — but the default is software you can read.

  3. 03

    No metering, no seats, no hosts

    I charge for days worked. Nothing I build bills you for growing, and there is no component that phones home to Helius or counts your servers.

  4. 04

    Handover is included

    Documented architecture with the reasoning, runbooks per alert, and a working session where your team drives. Not an upsell, not a final invoice line.

  5. 05

    The exit clause

    Stop the engagement whenever. No notice designed to trap you, no exit fee, no migration project. Everything keeps running because none of it needed me to keep running.

  6. 06

    Upstream where it helps

    Fixes and tooling that are generally useful go back to the projects they came from, or get released standalone — with your permission, and never containing anything of yours.

The stack I recommend to clients is the stack I run myself, and the pieces I have had to write because they did not exist yet ship open source for the same reason everything else does: software you cannot audit has no business running as root on your servers.

Why there is no proprietary layer

The commercial logic of a closed layer is obvious: it makes leaving expensive, which makes renewal easy. It works. It is also the reason so many teams are running infrastructure they cannot fully inspect, paying per host for the privilege, and quietly dreading the day the licence terms change.

I have been the engineer on the receiving end of that, and the experience is what shaped this. When an agent runs as root on every machine you own, "trust us" is not an acceptable answer to what it does. You should be able to read it, build it, and replace it.

So the business here is my time, not access to something. That has a real trade-off, and it is fair to name it: I cannot show you a slick platform with my logo on it, and I do not have recurring licence revenue smoothing out the months. What you get instead is infrastructure that keeps working when the invoices stop, which I think is the better deal for you and an honest one for me.

The practical test is simple. A month after I stop working with you, is anything degraded? If the answer could be yes, I have built the wrong thing.

The difference

Most infrastructure consulting sells you a dependency

Not maliciously, usually. It is just what happens when the deliverable is access to a platform rather than working code in your repository. Here is the distinction, stated plainly enough to hold me to it.
