Delivery pipeline

What a deploy actually looks like when it is built properly

Seven stages from commit to production. Drag the handle or use the arrow keys to see what happens at each one — and what it costs you when that stage is missing.

Drag, click a stage, or use ← → keys

01

Commit

instant

A push to a branch triggers everything. No manual button, no "remember to also run the deploy job" — the git history is the source of truth for what should be running.

Deploys depend on someone remembering a sequence of steps.

on:
  push:
    branches: [main]
  pull_request:
02

Build

~90s

Reproducible builds with real caching. The same commit produces the same artefact, and the cache keys are derived from lockfiles rather than guessed, so a cache hit is actually correct.

Forty-minute builds that re-download the internet every run.

- uses: actions/cache@v4
  with:
    path: ~/.cargo
    key: cargo-${{ hashFiles('Cargo.lock') }}
03

Test

~3m

Unit and integration tests run in parallel against ephemeral dependencies spun up as services. A failure names the test and prints the relevant log, rather than dumping 2000 lines and letting you search.

A red build nobody can diagnose, so it gets re-run until it passes.

services:
  postgres:
    image: postgres:17-alpine
    options: >-
      --health-cmd pg_isready
04

Scan

~40s

Dependency and image scanning, plus an SBOM generated and attached to the artefact. The gate fails on new high-severity findings, not on the pre-existing backlog — otherwise everyone learns to skip it.

You find out about the CVE from a customer’s security questionnaire.

- uses: aquasecurity/trivy-action@master
  with:
    severity: 'HIGH,CRITICAL'
    ignore-unfixed: true
05

Sign

~10s

The artefact is signed with a keyless workflow identity and pushed to the registry. Production only admits images whose signature it can verify, which makes "how did that get deployed?" an answerable question.

Any image that reaches the registry can reach production.

- run: |
    cosign sign --yes \
      ${REGISTRY}/app@${DIGEST}
06

Deploy

~2m

Progressive rollout with health gates. Traffic shifts a slice at a time, and the rollout aborts itself if error rates move — so a bad release is a non-event rather than an incident.

All-or-nothing deploys, and rollback is a manual scramble.

strategy:
  canary:
    steps:
      - setWeight: 10
      - pause: { duration: 5m }
      - setWeight: 50
07

Observe

continuous

The deploy annotates your dashboards and the alerts know which release they belong to. When something degrades three hours later, the first question — "what changed?" — already has an answer.

Correlating an incident with a release becomes archaeology.

- run: |
    curl -XPOST "$GRAFANA/api/annotations" \
      -d "{\"tags\":[\"deploy\"],\"text\":\"$SHA\"}"

Frequently asked

The questions people actually ask

Including the awkward ones about price and how to get rid of me.

How do engagements usually work?

Most start with a paid scoping conversation — a couple of hours where I look at what you have and tell you what I would do. After that it is either a fixed-scope project with a defined deliverable, or a retainer for a set number of days a month. I do not do open-ended time-and-materials without a scope; it is bad for both of us.

Who owns the code and configuration you write?

You do, entirely. Everything lands in your repositories under a licence you choose. There is no Helius-branded agent, no proprietary control plane, no component that stops working if you stop paying me. If a piece of it is generally useful I may ask to open-source it separately — but that is a request, never a condition.

Why only open-source tooling?

Because I have been on the wrong end of the alternative. Closed agents you cannot audit, per-host pricing that punishes you for growing, and vendors who change the licence once you are committed. Open-source components mean you can read what runs on your servers, host it yourself, and replace me without replacing your stack.

Do you take on-call or 24/7 support?

Not as a primary on-call rotation — I am one person, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. What I do is build the alerting and runbooks so your team can hold the pager competently, and act as an escalation contact during agreed hours on a retainer.

What access do you need, and what happens to it afterwards?

The least that lets me do the work — usually a named account with scoped permissions, never a shared root credential. Access is revoked at the end of the engagement and I will remind you if you forget. I do not copy production data to my machine; if I need realistic data for testing we generate or anonymise it.

Can you work with our existing team and tools?

Yes, and that is usually the point. I am not interested in replacing a working setup for the sake of my preferences. If you already run GitLab, or Nomad instead of Kubernetes, or a managed database, we work with it. I will tell you plainly if I think something is a genuine liability, and then it is your call.

What does it cost?

Day rate, quoted per engagement once I know the scope, and it is on the invoice rather than buried in a platform subscription. Scoping conversations are billed at the same rate. Email me with a rough description and I will give you a number and a range for how many days I think it takes.

How do we end it?

You say so. There is no notice period designed to trap you and no exit fee. Handover is part of every engagement rather than an upsell: documented architecture, runbooks, and a session where your team drives while I answer questions. The test is whether everything still runs a month after I stop — and it does, because none of it depends on me.

Tell me what is broken

A paragraph about what hurts is enough to start. I will reply with what I think it actually is, roughly how long it takes, and whether you need me at all — that last answer is sometimes no, and I would rather say it early.
